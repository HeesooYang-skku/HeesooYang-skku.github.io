About / Practice

Rather than separating art-making from research, I am interested in how images, texts, records, and interfaces become ways of sensing what is absent, repeated, or overlooked.

Artist Statement

My practice often begins with small traces: an image that has faded, a sound that is heard before it is seen, a record that cannot fully restore what it tries to hold. I am interested in the gap between presence and absence, and in the way media repeatedly translates experience into another form.

Through video, printing, scanning, writing, and installation, I think about how memory becomes material. I am less interested in perfect preservation than in the distortion, delay, and residue that appear during the process of recording.

In this portfolio project, that interest moves into data. Exhibition titles are collected, classified, and visualized, but the goal is not only efficiency. The dashboard asks how institutions name exhibitions, which concepts return again and again, and how viewers are guided before they encounter the artworks.

Exhibition Title Language Dashboard

What words appear before the audience sees the work?

This project analyzes the language used in Korean contemporary art exhibition titles. Instead of simply listing exhibitions by venue or date, it focuses on repeated words, language types, main keywords, and conceptual categories.

The project began from a question that connects directly to my position as a Fine Art student: how does an exhibition introduce itself before the artwork is encountered? A title is often the first text viewers read. It can frame the exhibition through ideas such as memory, body, time, space, image, archive, nature, and relationship.

The dashboard collects exhibition data from art institutions and turns it into an interactive interface where users can compare venues, filter language types, and observe recurring concepts. It approaches the exhibition title as both data and discourse.

How users move through the app

Users first see a general overview of the dataset, including the total number of exhibitions and venues. They can then filter by year, venue, language type, or keyword category.

After filtering, the charts update to show how the language of exhibition titles changes across institutions. Finally, users can check the original data table and source URLs.

Why this matters

Contemporary art exhibitions often use poetic or abstract language. By classifying titles into broader conceptual categories, the dashboard makes repeated institutional patterns visible.

The project does not claim that charts can fully explain art. Instead, it uses charts as an entry point for asking better questions about how art is named, introduced, and framed.

Methodology

The project combines manual interpretation and computational organization. This is important because exhibition titles are often metaphorical, multilingual, and context-dependent.

Keyword Classification

Because exhibition titles often use poetic language, I did not only count words mechanically. Similar expressions are grouped into broader categories.

Memory: memory, recollection, record, trace
Body: body, sensation, skin, embodiment
Time: time, future, past, present
Space: space, place, site, room
Archive: archive, record, collection
Relationship: relation, connection, in-between, together

Research Questions

The dashboard is organized around questions that connect data visualization with contemporary art interpretation.

Questions

What words appear most often in contemporary art exhibition titles?
Do institutions use more Korean-only, English-only, or mixed-language titles?
Are certain concepts more common in specific museums or galleries?
Do concepts such as memory, body, archive, and space appear repeatedly?
How do titles function as institutional language before the artwork is encountered?

Expected Interpretation

Exhibition titles are not just labels but part of how institutions produce meaning.
Repeated keywords can reveal trends in contemporary art discourse.
Language choice may suggest how institutions position themselves locally and internationally.
The dashboard can become a starting point for further curatorial or art historical research.

Development Process

The project developed through AI-assisted prompting, revision, sample data, interactive charts, and exhibition research.

Step 1

Basic layout + sample data

I created the first Streamlit structure with a title, introduction, sample exhibition dataset, and table display. At this stage, the topic was still close to a general art data dashboard.

Step 2

Charts + interactivity

I added interactive filters and charts for word frequency, language type distribution, and venue comparison. The project became more specific as I focused on exhibition titles rather than exhibitions in general.

Step 3

Polish + research framing

I refined keyword categories, improved the interface, and connected the project to my own art research on records, language, and institutional framing.

Prompt Development

The first prompt asked for a simple one-page portfolio website. After that, I revised the direction so that the website would not only introduce myself, but also explain the final project as an art research project.

The prompt developed from a general portfolio request into a more specific instruction: to reflect my Fine Art background, my interest in archives and absence, and the actual structure of the Exhibition Title Language Dashboard.

Version-up Strategy

The design was revised to include more sections: artist statement, project background, methodology, dashboard preview, research questions, development process, reflection, and future improvements.

This makes the page function as both a personal portfolio and a final project presentation page.

“The title is not only information. It is the first frame that appears before the exhibition itself.”

Through this project, I learned that data visualization can become a way of reading contemporary art language. The dashboard does not replace close reading or curatorial interpretation, but it helps reveal repeated patterns that may be difficult to notice one by one.

For future development, I would like to expand the dataset to include more institutions, compare changes by year, and add more refined keyword categories. I also want to connect the dashboard to my broader artistic interest in archives, memory, and the unstable relationship between records and absence.
